
**The short answer: for most companies, an independent senior studio delivers the best value — agency-grade work without agency overhead. Hire an agency when you need parallel teams and broad capacity. Hire in-house when the web is your product and work never stops.** The wrong choice is expensive in both directions: agencies overcharge for simple projects, and under-resourced freelancers underdeliver complex ones.

## The three options, honestly

### Freelancer

One person doing the work. Cheapest, fastest to start, and when it is a genuinely senior one, the accountability is perfect — there is nowhere to hide.

The risk is capacity and coverage. One person cannot run design, front-end, back-end, motion and infrastructure in parallel on a large project. When they get sick, sick days become your delay. The market also ranges enormously: a $500 freelancer and a $5,000 freelancer both answer "yes" to the same brief.

### Agency

A coordinated team: strategists, designers, developers, project managers. Broad capacity, parallel workstreams, process you can lean on.

The cost is coordination. Every hour of the team is billed with layers on top — account management, business development, overhead. For a straightforward marketing site, you often pay agency-grade money for the same senior hours a freelancer would bill directly. And the person who sold you the project is frequently not the person who builds it.

### In-house

A permanent employee who lives in your product. Unmatched context continuity and instant availability.

The cost is fixed whether the pipeline is full or empty, and one person rarely covers the full stack of skills a serious site needs. For most companies, web work arrives in projects — a launch, a rebuild, a campaign — not as a steady weekly stream that justifies a full-time salary.

## The questions that decide it

Skip the generic advice and answer these:

**How continuous is the work?** If the web is central to your product and ships weekly, in-house wins. If work arrives as defined projects, an external specialist is cheaper and usually better.

**How much breadth do you need simultaneously?** Brand strategy, UX, front-end engineering, back-end, motion, infrastructure — if a single project needs all of these *at once*, you need a team. If they sequence, one senior operator can cover it.

**Who is accountable for the outcome?** With an agency, ask who owns delivery when it slips. With a freelancer, ask what happens if they disappear mid-project. With in-house, ask who sets the quality bar. Vague answers here predict bad launches.

**What is the real budget?** If the budget is under about $25,000 for a marketing site, agency overhead will consume a visible share of it. That is not automatically wrong — but it should be a decision, not an accident.

## The middle path most people miss

There is a fourth option that rarely gets named: the **independent studio of one** — a single senior operator running their own practice. You get direct accountability (nowhere to hide), senior output (they do the work themselves), and no coordination tax (no account layer between you and the builder).

## How to evaluate whoever you hire

Regardless of the path, ask every candidate:

- **Show me something live you built.** Not a case study deck — a URL in production. Then ask what broke and what they learned.
- **Who exactly does the work?** If the answer is "our team," ask which team and whether the person in the pitch is on it.
- **What is out of scope, in writing?** Fixed scope with defined exclusions beats hourly rates every time.
- **What happens after launch?** Handover, documentation, maintenance. A site with no owner after launch is a depreciating asset.
- **How do you handle a scope change?** The answer tells you whether change orders are a business model or an exception.
